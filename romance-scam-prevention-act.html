The Romance Scam Prevention Act

The new US law tells dating platforms what to do after they ban a scammer. It leaves open the decisions that determine whether anyone is actually protected.

Online Security Bureau  ·  28 October 2026  ·  5 min read

Law
Romance Scam Prevention Act (H.R. 2481)
Signed
20 October 2026
In force
20 October 2027
Applies to
Online dating services delivered by website or mobile app
Enforced by
Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general

01What the law requires

When an online dating service suspends or terminates an account because it judges there is a significant risk the member will try to obtain money from other members through fraud, the service must notify every member who received messages from that account.

The notification must:

  • identify the banned account and the most recent time the two members exchanged messages;
  • state that the account may have been using a false identity or attempting to defraud members;
  • advise the member not to send money or personal financial information to other members;
  • provide fraud-prevention guidance and a customer-service contact.

The notice is due within 24 hours of the ban. A platform may take up to three days where it judges the circumstances require it, and must hold the notice longer where law enforcement requests a delay for an ongoing investigation. Violations are treated as unfair or deceptive practices under the FTC Act, and state attorneys general may also bring civil actions.

02Why it matters

The Act places a federal duty on dating platforms toward the people a scammer has already reached. That matters because by the time an account is banned, the conversation has often moved to WhatsApp, Telegram or another channel. Without notice, the person on the other end has no way of knowing that the account which introduced them to their contact was removed for fraud.

The notice follows the victim even after the conversation has left the platform. The Act passed the Senate by unanimous consent — a clear signal that platform responsibility for social engineering fraud is no longer a contested idea.

03What the law leaves open

The Act regulates what happens after a ban. It is silent on four things that decide whether the notice protects anyone.

1 · The quality of the ban decision

A notice is only as good as the decision that triggers it. Bans that come too late reach people who have already paid. Bans that are too broad produce false warnings, and false warnings teach users to ignore real ones.

2 · The returning operator

A ban attaches to an account, not to a person. An operator who registers again starts clean, and messages from the new account trigger nothing. The law applies account-level enforcement to an operator-level threat.

3 · Platform boundaries

The duty covers dating services only. Operators who first contact targets on social media, messaging apps or professional networks are outside it. And each platform notifies only its own members: a ban on one service tells nothing to another service where the same operator is still active.

4 · The money threshold

The trigger is tied to the risk of obtaining money. Social engineering harm often begins long before any financial request — through emotional manipulation, isolation from family and friends, or the extraction of intimate images later used for extortion. Those stages fall outside the trigger.

04The behavioural reading

OSB's RSEI Model™ reads social engineering as a sequence: relationship acceleration, script consistency, emotional manipulation and incremental financial extraction. A fraud ban notification will usually arrive late in that sequence — after trust has formed, after emotional investment, and in many cases after the first payments.

That changes who reads the notice. It is often not a neutral user weighing a safety tip, but a person who believes the relationship is real. The predictable first responses are disbelief, defence of the contact, and telling the contact about the warning. Platforms should also expect operators to anticipate the notice and frame it in advance — as a technical error, as the platform's bias, or as a reason to keep talking somewhere else.

The law prescribes what the notice must say. It does not prescribe how it says it — and tone will decide whether it works.

A notice that works is factual, free of blame, and gives one concrete next step: verify the person independently, talk to someone you trust, report what has happened. A notice that forces the reader to choose between the platform and the person they love will often lose that choice.

05Twelve months to compliance: priorities for platforms

  1. Define the ban standard. Document consistent, reviewable criteria for what the platform treats as a significant risk of fraud. Every notice now rests on that decision.
  2. Build recipient tracing. Identify every member who received messages from a banned account, with timestamps, inside the 24-hour window.
  3. Write the notice for the reader it will reach. Test the wording with people who have experienced social engineering fraud, not only with legal and product teams.
  4. Plan for law-enforcement holds. Create a process to pause a notice on request and release it on time when the hold ends.
  5. Plan for the return. Decide how banned operators will be recognised when they come back under a new account.

Compliance with the letter of the Act is a notification system. Protection depends on the decisions made upstream of it.

06OSB position

OSB welcomes the Romance Scam Prevention Act as a first federal floor for platform responsibility. The notification duty is a necessary step; it is not, on its own, a detection system, and it reaches only part of the social engineering landscape.
