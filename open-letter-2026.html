Letter to the industry · October 2026 · After the Act

The question has changed.

John Dee · Founder & Director, OSB · Published October 2026

To the leadership of every dating and social platform: your Trust & Safety, product, legal and compliance teams, and the executives who sign off on your safety posture.

In April, OSB wrote to you and asked a question: is your platform prepared to meet a real standard for social engineering fraud? On 20 October 2026, the United States answered part of that question for you. The Romance Scam Prevention Act (H.R. 2481) is now law. From 20 October 2027, dating platforms that ban a fraudster owe a duty to the people that fraudster has already reached.

This letter is about what that law requires, what it leaves open, and what it will take for your response to protect anyone. Our April letter remains on the record, unchanged: An Open Letter to the Platforms Where Romance Fraud Begins.

What the law now requires

When an online dating service bans an account because it judges there is a significant risk that the member will try to defraud others of money, it must notify every member who received messages from that account.

The notice must identify the account and the last time the two members exchanged messages. It must say the account may have been using a false identity or attempting fraud, advise the member not to send money or financial information, and give fraud-prevention guidance and a contact.

RequirementDetail
Who it applies toOnline dating services, by website or app
Deadline24 hours from the ban, up to 3 days where circumstances require
Law-enforcement holdsThe notice waits when an investigation asks it to
In force20 October 2027
EnforcementThe FTC, as an unfair or deceptive practice, and state attorneys general

This is a welcome first federal floor. The Act passed the Senate by unanimous consent. Platform responsibility for social engineering fraud is no longer a contested idea.

What the law leaves to you

The Act regulates what happens after a ban. It is silent on four decisions that determine whether a notice protects anyone.

  1. The ban decision. A notice is only as good as the judgement that triggers it. Ban too late and the notice reaches people who have already paid. Ban too broadly and false warnings teach users to ignore real ones.
  2. The reader. The notice usually arrives after trust has formed. The person reading it often believes the relationship is real. The law prescribes what a notice must say, not how it says it, and tone will decide whether it works.
  3. The returning operator. A ban attaches to an account, not a person. An operator who registers again starts clean, and the new account triggers nothing.
  4. The edges of the law. It covers dating services only, and it is triggered by the risk of financial fraud. Contact that begins on social media or messaging apps is outside it. So is harm that begins before any money is asked for.

The case the law does not reach

In April I told you about IFN-002. A person was targeted on a social platform. No money changed hands, not a single payment. The person still reached a point of suicidal ideation.

No financial loss. Maximum psychological harm.

Under the new law, that case triggers nothing. It began on a social platform, not a dating service, and the operator never asked for money. IFN-002 is why the RSEI Model™ carries the E Pillar Independent Trigger: when emotional manipulation scores 4 or above, OSB refers the person for support, whatever the financial picture. A safety system that waits for money to move will miss harm like this.

The ask

You have twelve months. Use them to build more than a notification system.

  • Define your ban standard now, so every notice rests on a decision you can defend.
  • Write your notices for the reader they will actually reach, and test them with people who have lived through social engineering fraud.
  • Decide how you will recognise a banned operator who comes back.
  • If you operate outside the United States, or outside dating, treat the Act as the benchmark your users will soon expect.

OSB works with trust and safety teams on ban criteria, notice design and operator re-identification. The RSEI Certified™ registry is still open, and no platform is on it yet. The first platforms to go beyond the floor will be the ones on the record when the law takes effect on 20 October 2027.
